Quantum-confined Stark effect

How an electric field reshapes the energy levels of an electron trapped in a quantum well

What is the quantum-confined Stark effect?

In quantum mechanics an electron is not a point at a definite position. It is described by a wavefunction, ψ(z), and |ψ(z)|² is the probability of finding the electron at position z. An electron that is free can have any energy. An electron that is confined can only have certain discrete energies, much as a guitar string fixed at both ends can only vibrate at certain frequencies.

A quantum well confines an electron in one direction. It is a thin layer of material, a few nanometres thick, in which the electron's potential energy is lower than in the layers on either side. Think of it as a trench of width L and depth V0. Because the trench is so narrow, only a few energy levels fit inside. The lowest is the ground state, with a single bump in ψ. The next is the first excited state, with one positive and one negative lobe. The wavefunctions leak a little into the walls, since the walls are finite and not infinitely high.

Now apply a uniform electric field F across the layer. The field adds a potential energy that rises linearly with position, eFz, so the flat floor of the trench becomes a tilted ramp. The electron slides towards the low side and its wavefunction becomes lopsided. The energy of the ground state drops, and the first excited state is pushed up. For weak fields the shift grows as F², which is the Stark effect. It is called "quantum-confined" because the walls keep the electron from simply escaping down the ramp, so the levels shift a lot without being smeared out. In a bulk crystal the same field would quickly pull electrons and holes apart and wash out the effect.

Second-order perturbation theory gives the size of the shift for a weak field. For an infinitely deep well the ground state moves by ΔE ≈ −(15 − π²)/(24π⁴) · m e² F² L⁴ / ħ². The steep dependence on well width (L⁴) is why wider wells respond much more strongly.

Why it matters

  • Electro-absorption modulators. The colour (photon energy) at which a quantum well starts to absorb light is set by the gap between electron and hole levels. A field shifts these levels, so a voltage can switch a well between absorbing and transparent at a chosen wavelength. The switching takes picoseconds, which makes these devices (and modulated lasers built on them) a workhorse for encoding data onto light in fibre-optic networks.
  • LEDs and lasers. In gallium-nitride (InGaN/GaN) blue and white LEDs the crystal has a large built-in electric field, a consequence of its polar structure. This field tilts every well, pushes electrons and holes to opposite sides, and lowers the emitted photon energy (a red-shift). It also reduces the overlap of electron and hole wavefunctions, so they recombine and emit light less readily. This lowers efficiency, and the colour drifts with drive current as injected carriers partly screen the field. Much LED engineering (thinner wells, non-polar and semi-polar substrates, strain engineering) aims to tame the QCSE.
